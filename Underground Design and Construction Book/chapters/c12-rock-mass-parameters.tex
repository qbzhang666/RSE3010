\chapter{Rock Mass Strength and Design Parameters}
\label{ch:rock-mass-parameters}

The parameters used in analysis describe a rock mass, whereas the measurements
available describe intact specimens and discontinuity surfaces. Bridging that
gap is the subject of this chapter. The generalised Hoek--Brown
criterion~\citep{hoek2019hb} provides the standard route, taking the intact
strength, a material constant, the Geological Strength Index and a disturbance
factor, and returning strength and deformability at rock mass scale. The
derivation is short; the behaviour it conceals is not. Small changes in the
index terms produce large changes in the derived strength, so the chapter
attends
to the choices that carry that sensitivity, the disturbance factor, the
fitting range and the modulus relation, as well as to the algebra itself.

\section{Deriving rock mass parameters}
\label{sec:deriving-rock-mass-parameters}

\subsection{The generalised Hoek--Brown criterion}
\label{sec:the-generalised-hoek-brown-criterion}

The generalised Hoek--Brown criterion for a jointed rock mass is
\[
\sigma_1^f =
\sigma_3 +
\sigma_{ci}
\left(
m_b\frac{\sigma_3}{\sigma_{ci}} + s
\right)^{a_{HB}} ,
\]
in which \(\sigma_1^f\) is the major principal stress at failure, \(\sigma_3\)
the confining stress, \(\sigma_{ci}\) the uniaxial compressive strength of the
intact rock, and \(m_b\), \(s\) and \(a_{HB}\) are constants of the rock mass.
The intact criterion of Chapter~\ref{ch:rock-mechanics} is the special case
\(m_b = m_i\), \(s = 1\) and \(a_{HB} = 0.5\). The generalised form departs
from it through two further inputs, the Geological Strength Index and the
disturbance factor \(D\), which between them represent the jointing and the
damage that separate a rock mass from a specimen.

\paragraph{Rock mass constants}
The three constants follow from \(m_i\), GSI and \(D\) as
\[
m_b =
m_i
\exp
\left(
\frac{GSI-100}{28-14D}
\right) ,
\]
\[
s =
\exp
\left(
\frac{GSI-100}{9-3D}
\right) ,
\]
\[
a_{HB} =
\frac{1}{2}
+
\frac{1}{6}
\left(
e^{-GSI/15}
-
e^{-20/3}
\right) .
\]
For \(GSI = 100\) and \(D = 0\) these return \(m_b = m_i\), \(s = 1\) and
\(a_{HB} = 0.5\), so the criterion reduces to the intact one. That limit is
the first check on any implementation of the three expressions.

\paragraph{Disturbance factor \(D\)}
The disturbance factor accounts for blast damage, stress relaxation and
excavation-induced disturbance around an opening, and it reduces both the
strength and the deformation modulus of the rock mass. Table~\ref{tab:Dfactor}
gives the values suggested for the common excavation methods.

\label{tab:Dfactor}

Increasing \(D\) lowers \(m_b\) and \(s\) together, and with them the rock mass
strength and modulus. Poor blasting can therefore raise the support demand
substantially in ground whose geology is unchanged. The factor describes a
damaged zone of limited thickness, however, and applying it to the whole rock
mass rather than to that zone understates strength everywhere beyond it; the
extent of the zone is discussed with the excavation methods in
Chapter~\ref{ch:construction}.

\noindent\textbf{Organising the calculation.}\enspace Whatever the medium, the
derivation should carry one line for each rock type and a separate block for
each disturbance case, typically \(D=0\) and \(D=0.8\), so that the effect of
blast damage is visible rather than buried. Every quantity should be computed
from the recorded inputs rather than transcribed, so that changing
\(\GSI\) or \(\sci\) propagates through the whole derivation. This is the
single most effective guard against the error described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:ground-modelling}, in which a parameter is revised in one
place and not in another.

Two of these are easily confused and must be kept apart. The first,
\[
\sigma_3^{IS}=\min(\sigma_v,\sigma_{h,k}) ,
\]
is the minor in-situ principal stress under the simplified two-stress
description, and it is a property of the site. The second,
\[
\sigma_{3,\mathrm{fit,max}}=\max(\sigma_v,\sigma_{h,k}) ,
\]
is the upper limit of the confining stress range over which the curved
Hoek--Brown envelope is replaced by an equivalent Mohr--Coulomb line, and it
is a modelling choice. The two take different values wherever the in-situ
stress ratio differs from unity.

\subsection{Rock mass strength and deformability}
\label{sec:rock-mass-strength-and-deformability}

Once \(m_b\), \(s\) and \(a_{HB}\) are known, the strength and stiffness of
the rock mass follow directly.

\paragraph{Uniaxial compressive strength of the rock mass}
Setting \(\sigma_3=0\) in the criterion gives the uniaxial compressive
strength of the rock mass,
\[
\sigma_{cm}
=
\sigma_{ci}\,s^{a_{HB}} .
\]
It is usually a small fraction of \(\sigma_{ci}\), and smaller still at low
GSI or high \(D\), which is the weakening effect of discontinuities and of
excavation damage expressed as a number.

\paragraph{Triaxial strength at in-situ confinement}
With the vertical and horizontal stresses
\[
\sigma_v = \gamma z ,
\qquad
\sigma_{h,k}=k\sigma_v ,
\]
and the minor in-situ principal stress \(\sigma_3^{IS}\) defined above, the
strength of the rock mass at that confinement is
\[
\sigma_{1m}^f =
\sigma_3^{IS}
+
\sigma_{ci}
\left(
m_b\frac{\sigma_3^{IS}}{\sigma_{ci}} + s
\right)^{a_{HB}} .
\]
This is a capacity and not a demand. It is the major principal stress that
would cause failure at the in-situ confinement, to be compared with the major
in-situ stress \(\sigma_1^{IS}\) and not mistaken for it.

\paragraph{Tensile strength of the rock mass}
The criterion gives a tensile strength
\[
\sigma_{tm}
=
-\frac{s\,\sigma_{ci}}{m_b} ,
\]
negative under the compression-positive convention. An alternative estimate
applies the Hoek--Martin tensile cut-off,
\[
\sigma_{tm,HM}
=
-\frac{\sigma_{cm}}{8.62+0.7m_i} .
\]
The two generally differ, and the difference is informative: where brittle
tensile cracking is the mechanism of concern, the comparison shows whether the
tensile strength implied by the criterion is credible or merely a consequence
of extrapolating a compressive envelope.

\paragraph{Deformation modulus of the rock mass}
The deformation modulus \(E_m\) is the stiffness of the jointed rock mass, as
distinct from the Young's modulus \(E_i\) measured on intact specimens. It
reflects discontinuities, weathering, blockiness, joint condition and
excavation disturbance, and it is ordinarily well below \(E_i\). It is a
principal input to deformation analysis, to support design and to numerical
modelling. Several empirical expressions estimate it. They were developed
from different data sets and different classification systems, they do not
agree, and each is an estimate rather than a material constant.

Where the intact modulus has been measured, the generalised expression of
\citet{hoekdiederichs2006} is the preferred estimate, because it is the only
one that uses the measured \(E_i\) together with the adopted GSI and the
disturbance factor:
\[
E_m =
E_i
\left(
0.02+
\frac{1-D/2}
{1+e^{(60+15D-GSI)/11}}
\right) .
\]
The result takes the unit in which \(E_i\) is entered.

Where a reliable \(E_i\) is not available, the simplified expression of the
same authors may be used,
\[
E_m~(\text{GPa}) =
100
\left(
\frac{1-D/2}
{1+e^{(75+25D-GSI)/11}}
\right) ,
\]
or 100\,000 times the bracketed term in megapascals. The exponent
\(75+25D-GSI\) belongs to this simplified form and \(60+15D-GSI\) to the
generalised one, and the two are not interchangeable. Because the simplified
form ignores the intact modulus it can substantially overestimate \(E_m\) for
weak rocks of low intact stiffness, such as mudstone.

An earlier expression estimates the modulus from GSI, \(D\) and the intact
strength,
\[
E_m~(\text{GPa}) =
\left(1-\frac{D}{2}\right)
\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{ci}}{100}}
\,
10^{(GSI-10)/40}
\qquad
(\sigma_{ci} \leq 100~\text{MPa}) ,
\]
with the square-root term omitted for stronger rock:
\[
E_m~(\text{GPa}) =
\left(1-\frac{D}{2}\right)
10^{(GSI-10)/40}
\qquad
(\sigma_{ci} > 100~\text{MPa}) .
\]
It serves where \(E_i\) is unmeasured but \(\sigma_{ci}\) and GSI are known,
and otherwise as a comparison; Appendix~\ref{app:constants} gives the
alternative of estimating \(E_i\) from the modulus ratio,
\(E_i = MR\,\sigma_{ci}\).

Older expressions take the modulus directly from a classification rating. For
better-quality rock masses the linear estimate of Bieniawski is
\[
E_m~(\text{GPa}) = 2RMR - 100
\qquad
(RMR > 50) ,
\]
and that of Serafim and Pereira, which extends to poorer rock, is
\[
E_m~(\text{GPa}) =
10^{(RMR-10)/40} .
\]
A corresponding estimate from the $Q$-system uses the normalised value
\(Q_c\),
\[
E_m~(\text{GPa}) =
10\,Q_c^{1/3} ,
\qquad
Q_c =
Q\,\frac{\sigma_{ci}}{100} .
\]
None of the rating-based expressions includes the intact modulus, the
disturbance factor or the excavation method explicitly. They are useful as
checks, and the $Q$-based form in particular where $Q$ has been logged more
reliably than RMR or GSI, but a design value rests on them only where nothing
better is available.

The order of preference follows from what each expression uses. The
generalised Hoek--Diederichs expression is the primary estimate wherever
\(E_i\), GSI and \(D\) are all available; the simplified expression is the
first comparison; and the remaining expressions are sensitivity checks. The
worked example of Section~\ref{sec:sandstone-worked-example} shows how far
they diverge in practice.

\section{Application and verification}
\label{sec:application-and-verification}

\subsection{Converting Hoek--Brown to equivalent Mohr--Coulomb parameters}
\label{sec:hb-to-mc}

Many design calculations and numerical models require the Mohr--Coulomb
parameters \(c\) and \(\phi\). Because the Hoek--Brown envelope is curved, no
single pair represents it; \(c\) and \(\phi\) are obtained by fitting a
straight line to the envelope over a selected range of confining stress,
\[
0 \leq \sigma_3 \leq \sigma_{3,\mathrm{fit,max}} ,
\qquad
\sigma_{3,\mathrm{fit,max}}=\max(\sigma_v,\sigma_{h,k}) .
\]
The fitted parameters are therefore not unique. A narrower range gives a
higher \(\phi\) and a lower \(c\), a wider range the reverse, and a fitted
pair is meaningful only with its range stated. The upper limit is a modelling
choice made to cover the confinement the analysis will encounter. It is not
the minor in-situ principal stress, although the two are easily conflated.

\paragraph{Method 1: Mohr circle regression}
A series of \(\sigma_3\) values is selected between zero and
\(\sigma_{3,\mathrm{fit,max}}\), and the corresponding \(\sigma_1^f\) computed
from the criterion. Each pair defines a Mohr circle of centre and radius
\[
C=\frac{\sigma_1^f+\sigma_3}{2} ,
\qquad
R=\frac{\sigma_1^f-\sigma_3}{2} ,
\]
and a straight line \(R=A+BC\) fitted to the pairs gives
\[
\phi=\arcsin(B) ,
\qquad
c=\frac{A}{\cos\phi} .
\]

\noindent\textbf{Fitting the line.}\enspace $B$ and $A$ are the gradient and
intercept of an ordinary least-squares fit and $R^2$ its coefficient of
determination. Any spreadsheet or statistical package computes them, as does
\companionname\ (Appendix~\ref{app:software}). The fitting range matters more
than the method: the resulting $c$ and $\phi$ describe the Hoek--Brown
envelope only over the confining stresses used to fit them.

\paragraph{Method 2: linear regression in \(\sigma_1^f\)--\(\sigma_3\) space}
The same data may be fitted directly as
\[
\sigma_1^f=a_{\mathrm{fit}}+b_{\mathrm{fit}}\,\sigma_3 ,
\]
from which
\[
\sin\phi=
\frac{b_{\mathrm{fit}}-1}
{b_{\mathrm{fit}}+1} ,
\qquad
c=
\frac{a_{\mathrm{fit}}(1-\sin\phi)}
{2\cos\phi} .
\]
The two methods weight the data differently and so do not return identical
values, but on the same points over the same range they agree closely. A
large difference between them indicates an arithmetic or range error rather
than a feature of the data.

\subsection{Worked example: the sandstone}
\label{sec:sandstone-worked-example}

The sandstone of Table~\ref{tab:inputdata} is carried through the whole
derivation. Its classification in Chapter~\ref{ch:classification} gave a basic
RMR of 60 with a groundwater rating of 7, hence \(RMR'_{89} = 68\) and
\[
GSI = 63,
\qquad
m_i=17,
\qquad
\sigma_{ci}=110~\text{MPa},
\qquad
E_i = 30~\text{GPa} .
\]
At a depth of 450\,m with \(k = 1.8\) the in-situ stresses are
\(\sigma_1^{IS} = 21.87\)\,MPa and \(\sigma_3^{IS} = 12.15\)\,MPa, and
\(\sigma_{3,\mathrm{fit,max}} = 21.87\)\,MPa.

\paragraph{Strength and modulus}
Table~\ref{tab:modulus_comparison} gives the derived quantities for an
undisturbed rock mass and for one damaged by poor blasting. For \(D = 0\) the
rock mass strength \(\sigma_{cm}\) is 13.95\,MPa, about an eighth of the
intact strength, and the strength at the in-situ confinement is 91\,MPa
against a major in-situ stress of 21.87\,MPa. That margin is a far-field
comparison and takes no account of the stress concentration at an excavation
boundary, which is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:ground-behaviour}.

The modulus estimates carry the lesson. For the undisturbed rock mass the five
expressions span 17.6 to 25.1\,GPa, a spread of about 40\,per cent that a
design can absorb. For the damaged rock mass the three expressions that admit
a disturbance factor span 3.1 to 12.7\,GPa, a factor of four, and the
simplified expression that was the highest of the three at \(D = 0\) is now
the lowest. The estimates do not merely scatter; they change order with the
conditions, which is why the expression adopted is the one that uses the
measured intact modulus and why the others are reported beside it.

Method 1 fits \(R = 3.85 + 0.673\,C\), giving
\[
\phi = \arcsin(0.673) = 42.3^\circ ,
\qquad
c = \frac{3.85}{\cos 42.3^\circ} = 5.2~\text{MPa} .
\]
Method 2 fits \(\sigma_1^f = 25.1 + 4.98\,\sigma_3\), giving
\[
\sin\phi=
\frac{4.98-1}{4.98+1}
=
0.666 ,
\qquad
\phi = 41.7^\circ ,
\qquad
c=
\frac{25.1\,(1-0.666)}
{2\cos 41.7^\circ}
=
5.6~\text{MPa} .
\]
The two agree to within a degree and half a megapascal, as they should.

Two features of the result deserve attention. The intercept of the fitted
line in Method 2, 25.1\,MPa, is nearly twice the true unconfined strength of
13.95\,MPa in the first row of the table: a straight line forced through a
curved envelope overstates strength at low confinement, which is exactly
where an excavation boundary sits. And the fitted pair belongs to the range.
Refitting over the narrower range from zero to \(\sigma_3^{IS} = 12.15\)\,MPa
gives \(\phi = 46.0^\circ\) and \(c = 4.3\)\,MPa, four degrees higher in
friction and a quarter lower in cohesion for the same rock mass. Equivalent
Mohr--Coulomb parameters are a linear approximation over a stated range of
confinement, and they are not material constants.
